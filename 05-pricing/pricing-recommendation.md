## 1. Pricing goal

- **Goal:** Enter a new market. Roamly Groups takes Roamly into group bookings, territory it has never served. The first job of the price is adoption: organisers must start group bookings, and the friends they invite must say yes and pay. The goal is **Acquire**: net-new travellers who come in through the friends each organiser invites. Margin comes second at launch.

## 2. Pricing model

- **Model:** Transaction-based. Value arrives one event at a time (one group, one celebration, a few times a year), not continuously, so a subscription would charge people for months they don't use it.
- **Who pays, and for what:** **Every friend pays their own share** of the experience price, plus a small **booking fee: a percentage of their own share, with a cap per person**. The fee pays for the group features: pay-your-share collection, the shared plan, and hosts ready for groups. The fee follows the value of the booking, so it stays small next to a cheap experience and doesn't balloon on an expensive one. Nobody pays a monthly fee and nobody commits in advance. The organiser pays the same as everyone else and no longer fronts the bill.


## 3. Packaging

- **Tiers:** Good-Better-Best, chosen per booking. Every tier includes the core: book together, everyone pays their own share, one plan everyone can see. Each person's fee is a percentage of their own share, capped, on top of the experience price.

| Tier | Who it's for | What's included | Rough fee (placeholder) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Good · Split** | A small group (4–6 friends) booking one experience | Pay your share · shared plan | ~3% of each share, max €3 per person |
| **Better ★ · Celebration** | The organiser of a celebration for 4–12 friends (birthday, hen or stag weekend, reunion) | Everything in Split · a group-ready vetted host · drop-outs don't fall on the organiser (unpaid shares are released, not charged to the organiser) | ~5% of each share, max €6 per person |
| **Best · Celebration Plus** | The organiser who wants the whole day handled, up to 12 friends | Everything in Celebration · a multi-experience day in one plan · a private host · planning help | ~8% of each share, max €12 per person |

★ **Celebration is the tier most organisers should pick.** It's built for the core audience, and it's where Roamly beats Airbnb Experiences, where one person pays for everyone.

**Why a percentage, not a flat fee:** a flat fee ignores how much the booking costs. A flat €4 is 20% of a €20 share but 2% of a €200 one, and a flat €9 would be 45% of a €20 share. A percentage keeps the fee in proportion, never above 8% of a share, and the cap stops it ballooning on expensive days out.

| Fee per person (effective %) | €20 share | €50 share | €100 share | €200 share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Split (3%, max €3) | €0.60 (3%) | €1.50 (3%) | €3.00 (3%) | €3.00 (1.5%) |
| Celebration ★ (5%, max €6) | €1.00 (5%) | €2.50 (5%) | €5.00 (5%) | €6.00 (3%) |
| Celebration Plus (8%, max €12) | €1.60 (8%) | €4.00 (8%) | €8.00 (8%) | €12.00 (6%) |

*The percentages and caps are placeholders to test, not research results. No Roamly price exists yet. The only market anchor is Airbnb Experiences: about $20–$200+ per guest, one payer per booking, and no group fee. The share sizes above are illustrative, chosen to span that range.* Illustration: a group of 8 on Celebration pays **€20** in fees on a €50-a-head experience (8 × €2.50), and **€48** on a €200-a-head one (8 × €6, capped).


## 4. Assumption to test

- **The one assumption I would test before launch:** Invited friends who don't know Roamly will pay their share **plus the fee** through Roamly without dropping out.
- **Why this one:** the whole plan depends on the invitee. Friends who have never heard of Roamly are the barrier the positioning names, and invitee conversion is the Acquire loop's primary metric. If invitees balk at paying an unfamiliar brand, or at the fee, the per-person model fails *and* the growth loop stalls. The fee would then have to move to the organiser, or the entry tier would have to be free.


## 5. One-page recommendation

**Recommendation:** launch Roamly Groups with a transaction-based model: each friend pays their own share plus a small fee (a percentage of that share, capped), in three tiers (Split · **Celebration ★** · Celebration Plus). Before setting any fee, test whether invitees will pay it.

### For Finance: *revenue arrives with every group booking, and the fee is set from evidence*
- Revenue scales with three things: the number of group bookings, party size and booking value. There's no subscription churn to forecast. Each group booking adds fee revenue on top of the experience itself (illustration: 8 friends on Celebration = €20 at €50 a head, €48 at €200 a head). The caps limit revenue on premium bookings. That's deliberate: it protects the organiser's trust where the bill is largest.
- The launch prioritises acquisition: fees stay low so invitees convert. The upside is new travellers who book again. Roamly's repeat booking rate is 3× the industry average, but nobody has measured yet whether invited friends follow that pattern.
- **Gaps to close before forecasting:** group-booking volumes, drop-off rates, the average experience price and Roamly's current take rate are not yet known. Payment-processing costs and the cost of releasing unpaid shares when someone drops out have to be modelled before the fees are fixed.

### For Sales Leadership: *the price sells itself in the product; the comparison that wins is price per person*
- Roamly Groups is product-led. The "sale" happens when an organiser books and the invite lands, so the price has to make sense on one screen with no explanation.
- Against Airbnb Experiences, compare the price each person actually pays, not the headline. On Airbnb the organiser fronts the whole bill and absorbs any unpaid shares. On Roamly each friend pays a few percent more on their own share, never more than €6 on Celebration, and nobody carries the risk.
- For partnerships and hosts: the fee sits on top of the host's price, so group bookings don't cut what hosts earn.

### For Product Marketing: *one story: "you pay just your part"*
- The fee has to read as the price of not being the one who pays for everyone, never as a surcharge. Lead with the organiser's relief ("this time, you don't pay for everyone") and the invited friend's ease ("pay just your part, in a couple of taps").
- Name the tiers after the occasion, not the features (Split · Celebration · Celebration Plus), and mark Celebration as the popular choice.
- **Gap:** the invited friend's message needs proof of payment safety and refund rules before launch. Without them, a fee from an unknown brand adds friction at the moment of trust.

### Rollout safeguards (a new price is a launch)
- **Localise, don't convert:** set fees by purchasing power in each launch city, not by exchange rate.
- **Existing travellers:** solo and small-party bookings stay exactly as they are. Groups is opt-in, so no grandfathering is needed.
- **Monitor before launch:** a dashboard of invitee payment completion, drop-out between invite and payment, and tier mix, with a rollback threshold agreed in advance. The threshold will be set once a baseline exists; none exists yet.

### Research method to test the assumption: **Van Westendorp**
The assumption is about how invited friends perceive a new fee. Van Westendorp's four questions (too cheap, a bargain, getting expensive, too expensive) give an **acceptable fee range** for a product that has no price yet, which is exactly the decision in front of us. Ask about the fee as a share of a realistic booking at several experience prices (e.g. €20, €50, €200 a head), so the result sets both the percentage and the cap. Recruit against the ICP: friends who have just been invited to a group booking, sliced by city and by whether they already know Roamly. Max-Diff (what goes in each tier) and Conjoint (fee-versus-feature trade-offs at scale) come later, once the fee range is known. One limit: Van Westendorp measures price perception, not trust. Pair it with a small pilot in one launch city that tracks real invitee payment completion.

## Slide 5: Business model and how HD makes money
- **Comment:** the cycle is complete, but the slide lacks the four numbers that decide the business: **funding cost, target yield, expected loss (cost of risk), and cost to acquire.**
  - **Target yield:** the interest and fees the book earns per year, as a % of the average outstanding balance.
    - It is lower than the headline APR because of early repayments, loans in arrears and promotions. The slide 6 APR (about 17.7%) is the price of one loan, not the yield on the book.
    - Since 20.11.2026 the APR cap (Law 5317/2026 Art. 40) puts a ceiling on it.
  - **Funding cost:** what HD pays per year for the money it lends: equity plus bank or wholesale lines, since it can't take deposits.
  - **Cost of risk (expected loss):** the share of the book lost to defaults each year, net of recoveries.
  - **Cost to acquire:** marketing, garage, dealer and broker commissions and onboarding costs per loan, spread over the loan's life.
  - **How they fit together:** yield − funding cost − cost of risk − operating & acquisition costs = margin, which drives RoE.
  - **Plain-words question:** *"What do you expect the book to earn per year, after early repayments and before losses? And how far is that below the legal APR cap?"*
  - The deck gives none of these four numbers. Don't quote any figure for them in the room.
- **Question (the big one):** "Where does the money come from after the €5m equity?" A credit company cannot take deposits. Greek banks sit on an LCR of 197.8% (BoG FSR, October 2026), so bank warehouse lines are plausible. What spread are you assuming?
- **Question:** "What cost of risk does the plan assume for unsecured micro-loans?"
  - The BoG reports a system NPL ratio of 3.4% in June 2026, rising.
  - Household NPLs were 8.6% (press).
  - Thin-file micro-loans will run well above the bank book.
- **Comment:** benchmark the ambition against the BoG H1 2026 sector figures: **RoA 1.3%, RoE 12.1%**. What RoE do they target for HDFS, and by which year?
  - **Peer benchmark** ([roe-benchmarks.md](../research/roe-benchmarks.md)):
    - Published returns for consumer lenders run from 13.3% to 24.2%, median 19.1%:
      - Bigbank RoE 13.3%
      - IPF 15.4%, target 15–20%
      - Santander Digital Consumer Bank RoTE 19.1%
      - tbi RoTE 20.9% (aggregator figure, unverified; public data only)
      - NOBA RoTE 24.2%
    - **Reasonable ambition: about 15–20% at steady state.** The floor is 12.1%, since investors will want more than the bank average for a riskier credit company.
    - Expect low or negative RoE while the book builds.
  - **Leverage point:**
    - Greek banks reach 12.1% on a 1.3% RoA because deposits let them lend about 9x their equity.
    - A non-deposit lender with equity at 25–40% of loans needs a **3–6% RoA** (after funding, losses, opex and tax) to reach 12–15% RoE.
    - With the APR capped, the size and cost of the bank lines decide the RoE.
    - **In simple words (illustration, round numbers):**
      - Shareholders put in **€100**. The bank adds about **€830 of deposits**, so it lends about **€930**, roughly 9.3x the equity.
      - It earns **1.3%** on the €930 after costs, losses and tax, which is about **€12**. That €12 on the shareholders' €100 is **about 12% RoE**.
      - The margin is thin, but it is earned on a large, mostly borrowed amount. 12.1% ÷ 1.3% ≈ 9.3x, using the BoG FSR figures.
      - **HDFS has no deposits.** Its lending beyond the €5m equity is funded by bank credit lines, so its leverage is **unknown**: the founders haven't stated it.
      - **Illustrative range of 2.5–4x:**
        - **2.5x is published.** IPF, a non-deposit consumer lender, holds equity at 40% of loans ([IPF 2025 CFO review](https://www.ipfin.co.uk/content/dam/ipf/corporate/documents/investors/annual-report-/2025/2025-ar-cfo-financial-review.pdf)).
        - **4x is my own assumption**, with equity at 25% of loans. It is not sourced.
        - At the banks' 1.3% RoA, 2.5–4x gives only **about 3–5% RoE**. To reach 12–15%, HDFS needs a **3–6% RoA**, and the APR cap stops it simply charging more.
      - **What decides HDFS's real leverage:**
        - how much of each loan the funding banks will finance, and how big a cushion of HDFS's own money they require (usually larger for a new, unsecured, unproven book)
        - any BoG capital rules for credit companies under Art. 153 of Law 4261/2014 (**not checked**)
        - HD's own risk appetite
        - later, securitisation or forward-flow sales, once there is a track record
      - **How to say it in the room:** *"I don't know your leverage, and that's what I'd like to understand. For reference, IPF holds equity at 40% of loans, which is 2.5x. How much bank funding have you lined up, on what terms, and how much equity does each euro of loans need?"*
  - **Question:** "What RoE do you target, by which year, and how much bank funding do you assume per euro of equity, at what spread?"
- **Question (insurance cross-sell, law):** "How does the credit-to-insurance cross-sell work under Arts. 15–16 of Law 5317/2026?"
  - The customer must be free to choose another insurer.
  - There is a 3-working-day wait before insurance can be sold alongside the loan.
  - So the LTV uplift can't rely on bundling at the point of credit. Ask whether the business case counts cross-sell revenue that the law would block.
- **Comment (margin under the cap):** with the APR capped and no deposits, the margin is decided by funding cost. That makes the funding question above the one that matters most.
