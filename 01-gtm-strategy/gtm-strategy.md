## 1. Discover Framework

### Align on the Goal
- **Core business problem:** Group demand already exists on Roamly, but the product is built for individuals and small parties. Users who try to book for a group work around it (separate accounts, manual split payments, syncing availability by hand) and drop off, so Roamly loses bookings it has already earned the demand for. The goal is to capture that demand and turn every group booking into a way to reach new travellers, without disrupting the solo experience that drives today's repeat rate.
- **What success looks like:** *Product:* booking for a group becomes a native flow, not a workaround, and group sessions stop dropping off. *Brand:* Roamly moves from "the best way to experience a city as a traveller" to "the best way to experience a city together". *Customer relationship:* the organizer stops being the group's unpaid admin and debt collector, and every friend they invite meets Roamly through a top-rated experience, not an ad. Support tickets about coordinating groups fall.


### Do the Research
- **Signals of a real opportunity:** (1) *Behavioural data pointing at unmet demand:* users coordinate group bookings across separate accounts, split payments by hand and sync availability themselves. (2) *Strong retention:* repeat booking runs at 3× the industry average, so the value is proven, but group sessions drop off at a high rate. (3) *Consistent qualitative feedback:* support hears the same thing over and over: "people want to do this on Roamly, but the product is getting in their way." (4) *Supply is ready:* 12,000+ vetted hosts across four continents, and the host network can support group bookings.
- **Top 5 possible segments:** 1. Friend-group organizers (4–12 friends: birthdays, stag/hen trips, reunions, friend getaways) · 2. Corporate team offsites and team-building (HR, office managers, event planners) · 3. Multi-generational family trips · 4. Wedding and milestone-celebration parties · 5. Clubs, alumni and university groups.
- **How to size the opportunity:** *What I need to know:* how many sessions today involve more than one account or traveller; how many of those drop off before booking; average party size × average booking value; how often an organizer books for a group; and host capacity for group sizes. *Where to look:* product analytics (multi-account sessions, cart abandonment by party size), support tickets tagged "group", payments data (bookings paid from several cards for the same slot), host data (maximum capacity, group pricing), plus external research on the group and friends-travel market for TAM.


### Build a Working Team
- **Cross-functional stakeholders:** Analytics (size the group-session funnel and the drop-off) · Finance (split-payment economics, unit economics per group, TAM) · Customer Support (the source of the signal; ticket themes and organizer pain) · Host / Supply Operations (group capacity, host pricing, host readiness) · Payments & Legal (split payments, refunds when someone drops out, liability) · Marketing & Community (travel-community and social channels, organizer advocates) · Product Design & Engineering (the group invite and payment flow).

### Form Your Hypothesis
- **Most valuable segment to target first, and why:** **Friend-group organizers.** These are existing Roamly travellers who plan a shared experience for 4–12 friends. They are the users already behind the workarounds, so the evidence points directly at them. Their pain is acute: they carry the admin and the money risk (they pay the full bill up front, chase friends for their share, sync availability across accounts, and lose the booking when people drop out). They also have the authority to buy: the organizer chooses and books the experience, and the friends agree and pay. Every group they book brings in friends who don't use Roamly yet, which makes this the segment where one launch delivers both conversion and acquisition.
- **Core value proposition:** Book a local experience for your whole group in one place: one invite, everyone pays their own share, and everyone sees the same itinerary, with no more fronting the bill or juggling separate accounts.


## 2. GTM motion
- **Chosen motion:** Hybrid: **PLG (primary) + CLG (secondary)**
- **Why:** On all five inputs, friend-group organizers land on PLG. *Time to value:* minutes (create a group, invite, split, book). *Buyer and user:* the same person (the organizer), with the friends peer-influenced. *Contract value:* low (consumer bookings). *Sales cycle:* self-serve. *Scales via:* product virality, since every group invite reaches 3–11 people who don't use Roamly yet. PLG is the engine: the group invite is the growth loop. CLG is the secondary layer because it is how Roamly already grows: current users discover it through travel communities, review sites and social media. Group trips are social by nature, so shared itineraries, group photos and reviews become content Roamly didn't have to make. This is the PLG + CLG pattern (community drives discovery, product converts), and the signal to watch is referral becoming a top channel.


## 3. Primary goal

**Acquire.** The goal is to bring in net-new travellers through the friends each organizer invites, so customers are reached *before* they have experienced Roamly's value. This matches the PLG primary motion (PLG fits Acquire, and CLG fits Acquire too). The organizer's own conversion matters, but it is the way in, not the goal.

## 4. The assumption I'm pushing back on

**The CMO's advice to "cut CLG at launch and prove the invite loop first."** I disagree. CLG isn't a new, slow-to-attribute bet for Roamly: it is the channel that built Roamly. Today's users find the platform through travel communities, review sites and social media, so the secondary layer reuses a discovery engine that already works rather than building one from scratch. Group trips also make community content naturally (shared itineraries, group photos, group reviews), so the community layer costs little and amplifies the product loop instead of competing with it. Cutting it would leave Roamly's best channel unused at the very moment the product becomes more shareable. To keep CLG accountable, it gets its own metric (below), so it isn't deprioritised under quarterly pressure.

*Accepted from the pressure test:* the opportunity still has to be sized (the working team's first task, see the sizing plan above), and the biggest risk is the CMO's blind-spot point: invitees are passengers, not buyers. If invitees don't go on to book on their own, Groups improves conversion but doesn't acquire. That is why the success metric below measures invitees, not organizers.

## 5. V1 GTM strategy one-pager

**Roamly Groups · V1 GTM strategy (first draft, to be refined over the coming weeks)**

**Launch thesis**
- *Who:* friend-group organizers, existing Roamly travellers planning a shared experience for 4–12 friends.
- *Why now:* the demand is already visible in the data (multi-account workarounds, manual split payments, high drop-off on group sessions), and the product and host network are ready.
- *Why Roamly:* best-in-category experiences, 3× industry repeat rate and 12,000+ vetted hosts. The first experience an invitee has is a Roamly experience, and that experience is what converts.

**Target segment (refined)**
Existing Roamly travellers who organise a group experience for 4–12 friends. *Launch focus:* celebration trips (birthdays, stag/hen trips, reunions) in the cities with the most group-session drop-off today. *Assumption to confirm with Analytics:* which 3–5 cities those are.

**Value proposition**
Book a local experience for your whole group in one place: one invite, everyone pays their own share, and everyone sees the same itinerary.

**Motion and goal**
PLG primary (the group invite is the growth loop) + CLG secondary (travel communities, reviews and social, where Roamly already grows). Primary goal: **Acquire**.

**Recommended channel approach**
1. *In-product (PLG engine):*
   - A "Book for a group" entry point on experience pages for parties of 4+.
   - Shareable invite links that work in the messaging apps groups already use.
   - An invitee page that shows the experience and lets each friend pay their share in a couple of taps.
   - Account creation tied to the paid share, followed by a post-experience "book your next one" prompt aimed at invitees.
2. *Existing base (seed the loop):* in-app and email messages to repeat bookers and to users whose behaviour shows group workarounds (multi-account sessions, several bookings on the same slot). Support routes group tickets straight to Groups.
3. *Community (CLG layer):*
   - Seed Groups in the travel communities and review sites where Roamly already grows.
   - Make shared itineraries and group photos easy to post.
   - Recruit organizer and host advocates, with hosts promoting their group-friendly experiences.

**Success metrics**
- *Primary (Acquire):* **invitee-to-booker conversion**, the % of invited friends who go on to make their own Roamly booking within 90 days. This is the metric that proves acquisition, not just conversion.
- *Leading indicators (PLG):* group completion rate (groups created → fully paid bookings), time to a fully paid group, invites sent per group.
- *CLG layer:* advocate-sourced bookings, meaning bookings that arrive through community, review or social referral links.
- *Guardrails:* solo-booking conversion and repeat rate stay flat (no disruption to what already works), and fewer group-coordination support tickets.
- *Warning sign:* lots of groups created but few fully paid (high sign-ups, low activation).
- *Targets:* to be set once Analytics has measured today's baselines. No baseline exists yet.

**Launch tier (suggested):** Tier 2, a focused GTM plan. The reach is a defined segment rather than the whole base, the impact is high, and there is real risk in saying nothing (users keep working around the product or leave). Revisit for Tier 1 once sizing is done.

**Open questions for the working team**
- How big is the opportunity: group sessions per month, the drop-off rate, and revenue lost per abandoned group?
- Do invitees convert at all? Test this early with a pilot cohort.
- Refund and drop-out rules for split payments (Payments and Legal).
- Host capacity and group pricing in the launch cities.
